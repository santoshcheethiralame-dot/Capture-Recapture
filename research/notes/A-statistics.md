## 1. Summary (the takeaways that matter for the project)

1. **Under heterogeneity, total N is not identified; only a lower bound is.** Link (2003) showed that heterogeneity models which fit the data equally well imply very different N. Holzmann-Munk-Zucchini (2006) showed that N becomes identified only if you restrict the mixing family (finite mixtures with enough occasions, beta, uniform), and that is an assumption, not something learned from data. Mao and Lindsay (2007) proved that for Poisson mixtures only one-sided (lower) confidence limits are possible. Consequence: every "estimated total number of features/attacks" should be labelled a lower bound plus a model-dependent sensitivity band.
2. **The pilot's headline numbers are exactly the lower-bound gap, not "estimator error".** I computed the population-level (noise-free) Chao2 bound for the pilot's scenarios. For logit-normal sigma=2, K=10 it is 0.82 N (pilot median Chao2: -18%). For Zipf alpha=1, K=10 it is 0.12 N (pilot: -89%). The pilot reproduces the theory to within a percent or two. Chao2 is behaving as designed; judging it against N is judging it against a target it never claimed to estimate.
3. **The pilot's recommendation (report coverage and "what 2x more runs would find") holds up, and I can now quantify it.** In the Zipf alpha=1 scenario, runs see 3-5% of N but Good-Turing/Chao-Jost incidence coverage is estimated at 0.416-0.420 against a truth of 0.416-0.420, and the predicted number of new items found by doubling K is within 5-10% of the truth for Zipf and 14-19% low for the heavy logit-normal. Coverage and short-range extrapolation are well-posed; richness is not. This matches theory (Orlitsky-Suresh-Wu 2016: prediction is possible out to t proportional to log n, and no further; Haegeman et al. 2013: richness cannot be estimated without unsupported assumptions about the tail, but Shannon and Simpson diversity can).
4. **Confidence intervals are the biggest thing the pilot did not test, and they fail in a specific way.** The standard log-normal Chao CI covers the lower-bound target 94-99% of the time but covers N itself 0-3% of the time under any real heterogeneity (sigma>=1, K=3 to 10; my simulation). A tight interval around a lower bound is dangerous if read as an interval for N. Report intervals as "at least N_LB (CI)" only.
5. **Ghost singletons (false non-matches) have opposite effects on N-type and coverage-type outputs.** The pilot showed Chao2 swinging from -14% to +43% with 15% ghosts. In my check, estimated coverage moves the other way: true 0.82, estimated 0.77 at 5% ghosts, 0.665 at 15% ghosts. Coverage errs on the side of saying "we found less than we did". That is the safe direction for a safety claim. The literature already has fixes for spurious singletons (Chiu and Chao 2016, Willis 2016 `breakaway_nof1`, Deng et al. 2024 warning).
6. **Covariates (Huggins/Alho) are powerful only when the covariate explains the catchability.** In my simulation the conditional-likelihood Horvitz-Thompson estimator is unbiased (+1%, -1%) when the covariate fully explains p, but drops to -32% to -45% with residual unobserved heterogeneity (tau=1 to 2), no better than Chao2. With a misspecified link on a power-law catchability it overshoots (+42%). Covariates help for "dependent on measurable things", not as a cure for unmeasured heterogeneity. Böhning's generalised Chao (2013) is the better-behaved covariate option because it keeps the lower-bound property.
7. **Dependence direction (two lists):** positive dependence makes Lincoln-Petersen/Chapman underestimate N; negative dependence overestimates, possibly wildly (Brenner 1995). Heterogeneity produces the same sign as positive dependence and cannot be separated from it with two lists (Chao et al. 2001). Nested SAE widths are the extreme positive-dependence case: if run A is a subset of run B, Chapman returns approximately |B| and conveys no information.
8. **For K>=3 lists with real dependence, log-linear/graphical/latent-class models are available but are identification-limited.** Aleshin-Guendel, Sadinle and Wakefield (Biometrics 2024) say that if no defensible identifying assumption exists, no estimate should be produced. Aleshin-Guendel (2020) shows the default LCMCR truncation is non-identifiable unless the number of lists is at least twice the number of latent classes, so with K=6 SAEs, use at most 3 classes.
9. **Best available benchmark with known truth (field data):** Grimm, Gruber and Henle (2014) found heterogeneity-aware models (moment estimator, interpolated jackknife, sample coverage / estimating equations in CARE-2) best across criteria, while covariate models and mixture models performed poorly. The 2025 evaluation of species-richness estimators (Schmitz and Rahmann) found Chao1/ACE/Chiu most accurate overall but strongly biased low in heterogeneous populations and when only 1-5% of the population is sampled. Both agree with the pilot.
10. **A hybrid and very close prior exists in two places.** (a) Software testing/fuzzing (Böhme): residual risk, discovery probability, Good-Turing and Chao estimators, with the explicit warning of adaptive bias, and the finding that jackknife was best but all estimators predicted "false peaks" (ICSE 2023). (b) A GitHub project, `ParitKansal/undiscovered-failures`, tests Chao1/ACE on planted failures in tabular ML and reports that the unseen share of errors is estimated without bias while the number of failure types is only a lower bound. A sibling repo reports a negative result for capture-recapture on MMLU-Redux label errors (calibrated but too-wide intervals). This should be cited and the project should state how it differs.
11. **Small K:** with K=2 nothing handles heterogeneity; with K=3-4 only f1/f2-based bounds (Chao) and sample-coverage estimators are usable, with high variance; iChao needs K>=4 (pilot also assumes this); jackknife of order 2 is unstable below K~5 and biased up under homogeneity (+13 to +16% in the pilot at K=10, sigma=0). Software-engineering reviews of capture-recapture in inspections (typically K=2 to 6 reviewers) converged on Mh-jackknife as the recommended default (Petersson et al. 2004).
12. **Practical reporting recipe (see section 6):** (i) coverage with bootstrap CI as the primary headline; (ii) "new items expected from 2x more runs" with CI; (iii) Chao2/iChao2 lower bound labelled as such; (iv) JK2 or ACE as a pessimistic-to-neutral comparison; (v) Huggins/Böhning only when a covariate has demonstrated predictive power; (vi) a sensitivity sweep over matching thresholds, reporting f1 and f2 explicitly.

**What it gets wrong or states imprecisely**

1. **It scores lower-bound estimators against N.** Chao1/Chao2 are derived as lower bounds (Cauchy-Schwarz, no assumption on detection rates). The right comparison for them is the *population-level* bound. I computed it (zero sampling noise, same scenarios):

   | Scenario | S/N | Population Chao2 bound / N | Pilot median Chao2 bias |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | lognormal sigma=1, K=5 | 0.61 | 0.83 | -17% |
   | lognormal sigma=1, K=10 | 0.79 | 0.92 | -8% |
   | lognormal sigma=2, K=5 | 0.59 | 0.72 | -28% |
   | lognormal sigma=2, K=10 | 0.71 | 0.82 | -18% |
   | Zipf alpha=0.5, K=10 | 0.30 | 0.82 | -19% |
   | Zipf alpha=1, K=5 | 0.01 | 0.07 | -95% |
   | Zipf alpha=1, K=20 | 0.05 | 0.20 | -83% |

   The match is within about 1-2 points everywhere. So the pilot demonstrates that the bound is a bound, not that the estimator is poor. The distinction matters for the paper's narrative: "Chao2 fails under heterogeneity" is the wrong sentence; "Chao2's guarantee is a floor, and the floor is far below N when the tail is heavy" is the right one.

2. **The "families" scenario (section 3) conflates dependence with heterogeneity.** In `families()`, the 1-family case shares a single individual effect u_i across all six occasions. That is just Mh with a larger variance (total sd about 1.58), not dependence between runs in the sense of an interaction between lists. The 6-family case gives each occasion its own independent u, which makes occasions *conditionally independent given z* and removes most heterogeneity. So the observed improvement from "diversifying the catchers" is a reduction of the total variance of individual logit-catchability, which is a real and useful design lesson but not the same as list dependence. True dependence needs a term that is not an individual effect shared by all runs: behavioural response (Mb: capture of feature i in run 1 changes its probability in run 2, e.g. a feature found early gets a prior in the next run) or pairwise list-specific interactions (log-linear two-factor terms; Fienberg-style). The pilot has neither. Negative dependence (runs deliberately steered away from each other, e.g. red-team agents told to avoid prior attacks) is also missing, and per Brenner (1995) it is the dangerous direction for two-list estimators because it overestimates, possibly extremely.
3. **No nested structure.** The proposal's Phase 1 trains SAEs of different widths. Widths are nested in expectation: a feature found at width w is much more likely to be found at 4w. That is neither Mh nor independent Mt. It is closer to a monotone threshold model. I added a quick threshold test (p_ij = sigmoid(k (u_i - c_j)), six cut-offs): median Chao2 error was -2%, -8%, -10%, -10% for k=1,3,10,30, JK2 within +7% to 0%, Darroch Mt -7% to -15%, and Mt diverged at k=30. Chao2 recovered only about 6 of the 16 points of N that were never observed at k=30. The conclusion is that the nested case is not catastrophic for f1/f2 estimators at mid-range, but the unseen mass *below the lowest cut-off* is invisible to every estimator, and this must be treated as an explicit blind spot. Note also the trivial case: if run A is exactly contained in run B, Chapman returns |B|.
4. **No uncertainty quantification.** Only medians of point estimates over 200 replicates. Medians hide the right-skew of Chao2 (f1^2 / f2 is heavy-tailed when f2 is small) and give no interval coverage. See my CI check in 2.0 below.
5. **Only the first two occasions are used for Chapman, and Mt Darroch is run in the presence of heterogeneity.** Chapman averaged over all pairs (or a Petersen-type multi-sample estimator, Schnabel) would be the fairer baseline; Darroch's Mt MLE assumes homogeneity across individuals and is expected to be low under Mh. These are intentionally weak baselines, which is fine, but they should be labelled as such in the README.
6. **N=2000 fixed; no small-N or small-sample regime.** For SAEs, N may be much larger (10^4-10^6); for red teams N may be 10-100. Chao's variance is dominated by f2 and breaks at small counts (it needs the +1 bias-correction, which the pilot does use). Run N in {20, 100, 500, 5000}.
7. **No coverage or extrapolation estimators** (Good-Turing/Chao-Jost, Chao 2014 extrapolation) even though the README recommends them. See my check below.
8. **`jackknife` for K=2** returns nonsense by construction (-52%), and `n/a` for iChao2 at K<4. Fine, but flag K<=3 results as "not defined", not as a bias number.
9. **Minor:** the README's claim "similar catchers act like heterogeneity" is better stated as "similar catchers increase the *variance* of the shared individual effect"; "spreading six runs over six families brings Chao2 to -2%" is the same as lowering sigma from 1.58 to 0.5, and the same -2% is visible in the table for sigma=0.5-ish homogeneity.

### 2.1 Basic two-sample and multi-sample estimators

- **Lincoln-Petersen and Chapman (1951) correction.** N-hat = (n1+1)(n2+1)/(m+1) - 1 is the nearly unbiased small-sample form. Classic, not re-fetched. Why it matters here: a two-run version is the simplest sanity check (two seeds, two red teams). Assumes independence and equal catchability; with heterogeneity the bias is N/(1+CV^2) (Chao 2001, see below). Leaves open: no uncertainty about assumptions, only the sampling variance.
- **Schnabel and Darroch Mt.** Multi-sample generalisations assuming occasion-specific but individual-equal capture probabilities. In Rcapture, "Darroch" is the log-linear Mt fit (Baillargeon and Rivest 2007, seen). The pilot's `darroch_mt` solves the likelihood equation directly. Why here: it is the right model when runs differ in strength (SAE of different widths or red teams of different skill) but features are exchangeable; it is wrong when features differ in catchability, and they will.
- **Otis, Burnham, White, Anderson (1978), Wildlife Monographs 62.** The M0, Mt, Mb, Mh, Mth, Mtbh family. Classic, cited in the proposal. Take-away for the project: in an AI-safety setting Mt (runs differ), Mh (features differ) and Mb (finding a feature changes later search) are all live. M0 almost surely does not hold. Mh (not Mb) is the natural default.
- **Burnham and Overton jackknife (Mh).** Linear combinations of capture-frequency counts. First order: S + f1 (K-1)/K. Second order: S + f1 (2K-3)/K - f2 (K-2)^2 / (K(K-1)). Implemented in the pilot. Petersson et al. (2004), a decade review of capture-recapture in software inspections (seen, abstract): theory comes from biostatistics, most software work is evaluative, and a majority recommended Mh-jackknife. Why here: the closest analogue to "several reviewers each find defects" and the only decade of AI-adjacent empirical guidance. Leaves open: the evaluations are mostly on small teams (K<=6) with known seeded defects.
- **Empirical estimator comparison on a known population (Grimm, Gruber, Henle 2014, PLoS ONE 9(6):e98840, seen).** Twelve estimators evaluated against reference population sizes built from field data on the arboreal gecko Gehyra variegata. Models that account for individual heterogeneity performed best on every criterion (bias, precision, accuracy, 95% CI performance). For heterogeneous habitats without obvious covariates: moment estimator or interpolated jackknife (CAPTURE/MARK); with plentiful capture-frequency data: sample-coverage or estimating-equation (CARE-2). Their multiple Lincoln-Petersen and truncated geometric (suitable for a long tail of low capture probabilities) did comparably well; covariate and mixture models performed poorly. Why here: the best published evidence on which families win when truth is known and heterogeneity is real. Leaves open: one taxon, real catchability structure not controlled.
- **Other known-population field studies (seen, snippets only).** A camera-trap study on 16 known elands (PLoS ONE 2015) reports the Mh Chao estimator beating Mh jackknife with sparse data; a cheetah study with known abundance found errors were overestimates for both jackknife and Chao, with Chao smaller. An older mammal study ("Evaluating population-size estimators: an empirical approach", J Mammal 1995, authors unverified) found jackknife the most stable in the first few trapping occasions. These conflict in direction because they sit at different sampling intensities; the lesson is that "which estimator wins" depends on f1/f2 and K, not on a universal ranking.

### 2.2 Chao's lower bound family and relatives

- **Chao (1987), "Estimating the population size for capture-recapture data with unequal catchability", Biometrics 43(4):783-791** (seen via review and software pages). Chao1/Chao2 are derived as lower bounds from a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality; they use only f1 and f2. Bias-corrected version: S + ((K-1)/K) f1 (f1-1) / (2 (f2+1)). It is used as a point estimate when an undetected item has about the same chance of being detected as a singleton. Chao and Colwell's retrospective, "Thirty years of progeny from Chao's inequality" (SORT 2017, seen), is the best single reference. Why here: it is the default for K between 3 and 10 and the only estimator whose failure direction is known by construction (always downward). Leaves open: no information on the tail beyond doubletons.
- **Chiu, Wang, Walther, Chao (2014), "An improved nonparametric lower bound of species richness via a modified Good-Turing frequency formula", Biometrics 70(3):671-682 (iChao1/iChao2)** (seen). Uses f3 and f4 to estimate the first-order bias of Chao's bound. The abstract did not report simulation numbers; the pilot already includes iChao2. Needs K>=4. In the pilot, iChao2 helps by about 2-10 points at K=5-10. Why here: the cheapest improvement for K=5-10 runs.
- **Chao, Colwell, Lin, Gotelli (2009), "Sufficient sampling for asymptotic minimum species richness estimators", Ecology 90:1125-1133** (seen). Gives an estimator of how much more sampling is needed for the observed richness to reach Chao1/Chao2's asymptote. Why here: it is a direct answer to "how many more runs?" for the lower bound, not for N.
- **ACE/ICE (Chao and Lee 1992, JASA 87:210-217)** (seen via SPECIES docs). Coverage-based estimator that separates "rare" (frequency <= 10) and "abundant" items and estimates rare-item heterogeneity with a CV term. ACE-1 adds a bias correction for CV>2 (seen in a practitioner guide, unverified source). The SPECIES package default cut-off is 10. For K<10 runs every item is "rare", which is a limitation: set the cut-off to K.
- **Chao and Bunge (2002), coverage-duplication estimator; SPECIES package (Wang 2011, JSS 40(9), seen).** Poisson-gamma model, estimates coverage and duplication. Also includes the penalised NPMLE (Wang and Lindsay 2005).
- **Zelterman (1988) and Böhning's ratio regression.** Zelterman uses only f1, f2 (via a truncated Poisson) and is robust to heterogeneity but poor for long tails (seen in the Rocchetti, Alfó, Böhning snippet). Rocchetti, Bunge and Böhning, "Population size estimation based upon ratios of recapture probabilities", Ann Appl Stat 5(2B):1512-1533 (2011), arXiv 1107.5423 (seen; author order unverified), and Rocchetti, Alfó, Holling, Böhning (2016, Biometrics) build a regression on ratios of successive frequencies (x+1) f_{x+1} / f_x. Why here: ratio plots are a free diagnostic of how misspecified a Poisson/geometric assumption is; they use f2 and above only, which helps when f1 is contaminated by ghosts (see Willis below).
- **Mao and Lindsay (2007), "Estimating the number of classes", Ann Stat 35(2):917-930, arXiv 0708.2153** (seen). In a Poisson mixture model the number of classes reduces to the odds that a class is undetected; discontinuity of that odds prevents locally unbiased informative estimators and restricts confidence intervals to be one-sided. Lower semicontinuity gives non-parametric lower confidence limits. Why here: the theorem that makes "lower bound only" a mathematical necessity.
- **Wang and Lindsay (2005), penalised NPMLE, JASA** (seen only as a citation). Boundary problem handling. Useful as an alternative to Chao in `SPECIES`.
- **Willis and Bunge (2015), "Estimating diversity via frequency ratios", Biometrics 71(4):1042-1049** (seen) and the `breakaway` R package (CRAN, updated 2026). Nonlinear regression on ratios of consecutive frequency counts, designed for high latent diversity. **Willis (2016), "Species richness estimation with high diversity but spurious singletons", arXiv 1604.02598** (seen): extends to predict both the unobserved richness and the true singleton count. `breakaway_nof1` does not need f1. Why here: the ghost-singleton problem in the pilot is the same problem; this is the existing statistical answer, and the package is in R.
- **Bunge and Fitzpatrick (1993), JASA 88:364-373 (review); Bunge, Willis, Walsh (2014), Annu Rev Stat Appl 1:427-445** (seen). Standard reviews. Bunge et al. is the best entry into parametric versus non-parametric approaches and the identifiability issues in microbial diversity.
- **Schmitz and Rahmann (2025), "A comprehensive review and evaluation of species richness estimation", Briefings in Bioinformatics 26(2):bbaf158** (seen; open access; code on GitLab). Evaluation across simulated compositions: no single winner; Chao1/ACE/Chiu give the most accurate point estimates for many compositions but strongly underestimate when the population is very heterogeneous or only a small portion (1-5%) is sampled; they overestimate for some compositions when more than 50% of the population is sampled; upsampling methods (PreSeq, RichnEst) predict richness in a sample up to 10 times larger; PreSeq is among the best at low sampling rates for power-decay and Zipf-Mandelbrot distributions. Caveat: no unclean data. Why here: the most recent head-to-head benchmark; its caveat is exactly what the project must add (matching errors).
- **Tekwa et al. (2023), "Theory and application of an improved species richness estimator", PMC10225866** and a Gamma-Poisson-based estimator on ResearchGate (seen; both authors unverified). Report reduced bias, RMSE and CI coverage versus Chao1 under heterogeneous detection. Candidate extra estimator, not validated by me.
- **Deng, Umbach, Neufeld (2024), "Nonparametric richness estimators Chao1 and ACE must not be used with amplicon sequence variant data", ISME J 18(1):wrae106** (seen). The denoising pipeline removes singletons, which both estimators need; keeping singletons adds artefacts that inflate richness. Why here: a clean example of a preprocessing step (here, thresholding or deduplication of "features") destroying the data the estimator relies on. Our matching threshold is an equivalent step.
- **Chiu and Chao (2016), "Estimating and comparing microbial diversity in the presence of sequencing errors", PeerJ 4:e1634** (seen). Low-frequency counts produce spurious singletons; R code on Chao's website. Di Cecco and Tancredi (2024, Environ Ecol Stat 10.1007/s10651-024-00614-w, snippet only; authors as seen) model spurious singletons as false-negative record linkage errors and find that the estimate of the total number of species is equivalent to deleting them. Why here: directly parallel to "false non-matches" and gives two ready corrections.

### 2.3 Heterogeneity models and identifiability

- **Link (2003), "Nonidentifiability of population size from capture-recapture data with heterogeneous detection probabilities", Biometrics 59(4):1123-1130** (seen; USGS record). Reasonable alternative models of heterogeneity can predict essentially identical observations from populations of substantially different sizes; even with very large samples, the analyst cannot distinguish among reasonable heterogeneity models though they yield quite distinct inferences about N. Illustrated for closed and open models. Why here (critical): it kills any claim that "the" total feature count can be estimated from SAE runs. Leaves open: my snippet did not give the numerical examples.
- **Holzmann, Munk, Zucchini (2006), "On identifiability in capture-recapture models", Biometrics 62(3):934-936** (seen; abstract). General criterion for identifiability of the mixing distribution, applied to finite and beta mixtures; covers binomial and geometric outcomes. A later paper summarising it (unverified wording): N is identified under uniform (with more than one visit), beta (more than two visits), or finite mixture with at least twice as many occasions as components. They also argue that the standard definition of identifiability can mislead. Why here: identification is *conditional on parametric family*; the data cannot choose the family; this is a reasoned restriction, not a discovery.
- **"On the nonidentifiability of population sizes" (PubMed 18844871, 2008)** (seen; first author unverified). A nonparametric mixture means N is nonidentifiable, but identifiable subfamilies give estimable lower bounds. Reinforces Chao as the principled option.
- **Pledger (2000), "Unified maximum likelihood estimates for closed capture-recapture models using mixtures", Biometrics 56(2):434-442** (seen). Finite mixtures (e.g. two-point) of capture probabilities over Mh/Mth/Mbh; implemented as the "h2" estimator in `secr::closedN`. **Pledger (2005), "The performance of mixture models in heterogeneous closed population capture-recapture", Biometrics 61(3):868-873** (seen; abstract) compares beta-binomial and finite-mixture models using simulations from many distributions to suggest when each is useful. **Dorazio and Royle (2003), "Mixture models for estimating the size of a closed population when capture rates vary among individuals", Biometrics 59:351-364** (seen, abstract-level) introduce the beta-binomial/infinite mixture and, with a rejoinder to Pledger 2005, argue about mixture choice. Morgan and Ridout (2008, JRSS C, "A new mixture model for capture heterogeneity", first author unverified). Why here: these are the "full-parametric" options; the debate itself (Link vs Pledger vs Dorazio-Royle) is the evidence that the answer depends on the family. Grimm et al. (2014) found mixture models performed poorly on their field data.
- **Link (2013), "A cautionary note on the discrete uniform prior for the binomial N", Ecology 94:2173-2179** (seen). Discrete uniform prior (what data-augmentation with large M implicitly uses) yields an improper posterior for N under some conditions; the scale prior 1/N is advised. Why here: if we use Bayesian data augmentation (Royle 2009 style, not retrieved this session), the prior on N determines the answer when f1 is large.
- **Haegeman, Hamelin, Moriarty, Neal, Dushoff, Weitz (2013), "Robust estimation of microbial diversity in theory and in practice", ISME J 7:1092-1101, arXiv 1302.3753** (seen). Sample data do not contain information about the number of rare species in the tail, so richness cannot be reliably estimated without unsupported assumptions about the species abundance distribution; Chao's estimator can invert the ranking of communities when many rare species are present; they construct lower and upper estimates for Hill diversities and recommend Shannon and Simpson diversity over richness. Why here: this is the right "metric downgrade" argument for the heavy-tailed SAE case. A caution: the authors' correct names are as stated here; a first guess at the author list from memory was wrong.
- **Chao, Hsieh, Chazdon, Colwell, Gotelli (2015), "Unveiling the species-rank abundance distribution by generalizing the Good-Turing sample coverage theory", Ecology 96:1189-1201** (seen). Corrects the relative abundance of detected species (the empirical rank-abundance overstates abundance of rare detected species) and adds an estimated profile for undetected species based on a lower bound. Simulations show it is more accurate than the empirical curve. Why here: the SAE equivalent of estimating the *activation-frequency distribution including unseen features*.

### 2.4 Covariates: Huggins, Alho and Horvitz-Thompson

- **Huggins (1989) and Alho (1990)** (classic, cited via the White model notes and the Schofield thesis in a search result; seen as citations). Conditional likelihood on being caught at least once, with logistic capture probability depending on covariates observed on caught individuals; N-hat is the Horvitz-Thompson sum of 1 / (1 - prod(1 - p_hat_ij)). The conditional likelihood drops unseen individuals, so no covariates are needed for them. In `VGAM`, `posbinomial` fits this (Yee, Stoklosa, Huggins, "The VGAM package for capture-recapture data using the conditional likelihood", JSS, authors unverified; seen on ResearchGate). Why here: SAE features have measurable covariates (activation frequency, mean activation norm, decoder-norm, max-activating example strength). A covariate that tracks catchability turns an unidentified problem into an identified one *if* the logit-linear (or additive) model is right in the region of the unseen items. My simulation shows the two ways this fails: residual heterogeneity (still -32 to -45%) and misspecified link on a power law (+42%). Leaves open: no sample-size rules; covariates measured post hoc after capture can be affected by capture (selection).
- **Böhning (2013), "A generalization of Chao's estimator for covariate information", Biometrics** (seen); **Böhning and van der Heijden (2009), "A covariate adjustment for zero-truncated approaches to estimating the size of hidden and elusive populations", Ann Appl Stat 3(2)** (seen; authors as seen, order unverified). Generalised Chao is equivalent to a truncated Poisson regression, asymptotically unbiased if covariates capture all heterogeneity; generalised Zelterman reduces to the usual Zelterman without covariates. Dennett and Böhning, arXiv 2312.08391 (seen; journal version 2025 in Statistical Methods and Applications, "Performance and robustness of single-source capture-recapture population size estimators with covariate information and potential one-inflation", seen) compare Horvitz-Thompson, generalised Chao and generalised Zelterman and include a one-inflation correction. Why here: keeps the lower-bound character while using covariates, and the one-inflation correction is relevant when "ghost" singletons make f1 too big.
- **Cruyff and van der Heijden (2008), zero-truncated negative binomial regression for population size, Biometrical Journal 50:1035-1050** (seen). Overdispersion from unobserved heterogeneity; the negative binomial gave a noticeably higher estimate than Poisson in the opiate-user example. Why here: model-based sensitivity band for single-list count data (counts of how many runs found each feature).
- **Das and Kennedy (2021), "drpop: Efficient and doubly robust population size estimation in R", arXiv 2111.07191** (seen). Assumes lists are conditionally independent given covariates, flexible nonparametric nuisance estimation, doubly robust. Why here: the modern semiparametric version of Huggins; two-list; if SAE covariates are rich (activation statistics, decoder cosine neighbours) this is the principled way to use them. Leaves open: needs conditional independence given covariates, which nested SAEs violate unless width is itself a conditioned-on covariate.
- **Stoklosa and others on residual diagnostics for closed-population models (Methods Ecol Evol, 2025–26 listing, title and year as seen)**: "Residual diagnostics for assessing closed population capture-recapture models" (seen as a link only; unverified details). A diagnostic route for checking Huggins models.

### 2.5 Dependence between lists

- **Fienberg, Johnson, Junker (1999), "Classical multilevel and Bayesian approaches to population size estimation using multiple lists", JRSS A 162(3):383-405** (seen). Heterogeneity as a restricted form of interaction among lists in the contingency table; connects the Rasch model to log-linear models (quasi-symmetry); Bayesian MCMC variants. **Darroch, Fienberg, Glonek, Junker (1993), JASA 88:1137-1148, "A three-sample multiple-recapture approach to census population estimation with heterogeneous catchability"** (seen as a citation). Why here: log-linear models are the default for K=3..6 lists in epidemiology and human-rights counting; K=3 is the minimum for a nonzero test of one pairwise dependence.
- **Chao, Tsay, Lin, Shau, Chao (2001), "The applications of capture-recapture models to epidemiological data", Stat Med 20(20):3123-3157** (seen). Reviews ecological models, log-linear models, and the sample-coverage approach; each handles local (list) dependence and dependence due to heterogeneity; the CCV (coefficient of covariation) measures dependence; with two lists, independence is CCV=0; heterogeneity-induced dependence is generally confounded with behavioural-response dependence. Program CARE. Why here: the cleanest statement of the confound that will plague any two-run result.
- **Chao, Lee, Jeng (1992), Biometrics 48:201-216 (Mth sample coverage); Lee and Chao (1994), Biometrics 50:88-97** (seen as citations). SC1/SC2 estimators for Mth, available in `secr::closedN` as `chao.th1`. Why here: the sample-coverage route to Mth, i.e. runs differ in strength *and* features differ in catchability, which is the SAE-width situation.
- **Brenner (1995), "Use and limitations of the capture-recapture method in disease monitoring with two dependent sources", Epidemiology 6(1):42-48** (seen). Negative dependence turns underascertainment by registration into *overestimation* by capture-recapture, possibly extreme; for positive dependence the correction is imperfect but underestimation is typically much less severe than with the registration alone. Hook and Regal (1995), Epidemiol Rev 17:243-264 (seen as a citation) is the review of limitations. Why here: gives the sign rules in the decision guide; the "extreme overestimation under negative dependence" reminds us that red-team runs told to diversify are a risk.
- **Madigan and York (1997), "Bayesian methods for estimation of the size of a closed population", Biometrika 84(1):19-31** (seen). Decomposable graphical models with Bayesian model averaging over parameter and model uncertainty. R implementation: `dga` (CRAN 2.0.2, 2025-09; function `bma.cr`; pre-computed model sets for 3, 4, 5 lists; seen). Why here: for K up to 5 groups of runs (e.g. SAE-width classes, red-team families) it integrates over the uncertainty in dependence structure. Leaves open: number of decomposable graphs explodes beyond 5 lists.
- **Manrique-Vallier (2016), "Bayesian population size estimation using Dirichlet process mixtures", Biometrics 72(4):1246-1254** (seen). Latent-class model with a nonparametric (Dirichlet process) number of classes, handles sparse tables with many lists. R package `LCMCR` (v0.4.11 cited). **Aleshin-Guendel (2020), "On the identifiability of latent class models for multiple-systems estimation", arXiv 2008.09865** (seen): the truncated family used in practice is non-identifiable when 2 J* > K (J* truncation point, K number of lists); the default LCMCR truncation implies non-identifiability unless one has K>=10 sources. Why here: it says that with K=6 runs, LCMCR as shipped is not identified; set J* <= 3 and treat results as sensitivity analysis.
- **Aleshin-Guendel, Sadinle, Wakefield (2024), "The central role of the identifying assumption in population size estimation", Biometrics 80(1):ujad028, arXiv 2101.09304** (seen). Separates observed-data model from identifying assumption; models with different identifying assumptions can give very different estimates even when fitting the observed data equally well; if no appropriate assumption can be found, no estimate should be produced. Discussion pieces in the same issue (King, McCrea, Overstall) argue models may perform poorly in practice (seen). Code: github.com/aleshing/central-role-of-identifying-assumptions. Why here: the formal framing for "what is the project's identifying assumption, and what is its sensitivity analysis".
- **Chan, Silverman, Vincent (2021), "Multiple systems estimation for sparse capture data: inferential challenges when there are nonoverlapping lists", JASA 116(535):1297-1306, arXiv 1902.05156** (seen). Poisson log-linear regression for sparse data, existence and identifiability of MLEs; non-identifiability arises when all list pairs are in the model and every set of three lists contains a non-overlapping pair; standard GLM software does not check for non-existence. R package `SparseMSE`. Follow-up on bootstrapping model selection (Silverman, Chan, Vincent 2023, arXiv 2303.17856, seen). Why here: SAE features found by exactly one of many runs yield sparse tables with many empty overlaps; this is the exact regime.
- **Other MSE references seen:** Vincent, Sharifi Far, Papathomas (2023), "Common methodological challenges encountered with multiple systems estimation studies" (seen); "On the reliability of multiple systems estimation for the quantification of modern slavery", arXiv 2112.01594 (seen as a link; contents not read); Silverman's Annual Review on MSE for public policy (seen as a link; authors/year unverified); arXiv 2407.03539 "Population size estimation with many lists and heterogeneity: a conditional log-linear model among the unobserved" (seen as a link; not read); arXiv 2601.05859 "Neural methods for multiple systems estimation models" (seen as a link; not read). Mentioned as leads only.

### 2.6 Coverage, extrapolation, and the unseen-species problem

- **Good-Turing coverage.** Sample coverage C = 1 - f1/n (abundance data) or the incidence form given above. Good (1953) and Good and Toulmin (1956), classic, not re-fetched. Efron and Thisted (1976), Biometrika 63:435-447 (seen): Shakespeare example; their conclusion that he knew at least 35,000 unused words, and a prediction for a text of equal size of 11,460 new word types with expected error under 150. Good-Toulmin is well behaved for extrapolation factors t<=1 but fails beyond t=1 without smoothing.
- **Orlitsky, Suresh, Wu (2016), "Optimal prediction of the number of unseen species", PNAS 113(47)** (seen; arXiv 1511.07428). A class of smoothed Good-Toulmin estimators provably predicts the number of unseen species up to t proportional to log n, and this range is the best possible. Minimax optimality (confirmed in a 2020 NeurIPS review of the follow-up). Why here: the theoretical answer to "how many new things would we find with more runs"; it says short-range extrapolation (2x, 3x) is well posed and long-range (100x) is not, without extra assumptions.
- **Valiant and Valiant, "Estimating the unseen" (NIPS 2013; J ACM 64(6), 2017)** (seen). Linear-programming reconstruction of the unseen part of the distribution; sample complexity n / log n for support size and entropy (STOC 2011 companion). Why here: the recipe to estimate the activation-frequency histogram including unseen features, for diagnostics. In Schmitz and Rahmann's benchmark, Valiant extremely underestimated richness when only a small portion was observed, so use it for the histogram, not for N.
- **Eriksson (2026), "The unseen species problem revisited", arXiv 2602.08769** (seen, v4 abstract). For small extrapolation m, Good-Toulmin is the unique estimator that respects the problem's symmetries and has non-trivial rate (v2 said minimax-optimal within a factor of 2), with principled prediction intervals (an open problem resolved); for intermediate m a new estimator with much better worst-case MSE; for large m, assuming a power-law tail, a simple estimator matching a recent sophisticated method's rate; results extend to incidence data with bounded set sizes. Why here: the most current theory including *prediction intervals* and *incidence data*, exactly our setting. Not read in full.
- **Lee and Böhme (2025, ICLR), "How much is unseen depends chiefly on information about the seen", arXiv 2402.05835** (seen). Missing mass in expectation depends on the count of classes seen exactly k times plus an exponentially decaying error; search for estimators with smaller MSE than Good-Turing (about 80% of Good-Turing's MSE, better in over 93% of runs when samples >= classes). Why here: a data-driven improvement of Good-Turing for the coverage headline.
- **Vipulanandan, Premaratne, Sarkar (2026), "Rarity of singletons matters: unseen-species and missing-mass estimation in small-sample regimes", arXiv 2610.22604** (seen; submitted 15 Oct 2026; abstract only). A fragility-aware correction to Good-Turing and Good-Toulmin when singleton counts are unreliable, compared against Chao, ACE and jackknife on 360 configurations. Why here: very recent, directly about unreliable singletons, which is our matching-error problem. Not read beyond the abstract; claims not verified.
- **Chao and Jost (2012), "Coverage-based rarefaction and extrapolation: standardizing samples by completeness rather than size", Ecology 93(12):2533-2547** (seen). Compare communities at equal sample coverage instead of equal size; stop sampling when coverage hits a target. A critique, Alroy, "Coverage-based rarefaction fails to quantify relative species richness", Oikos (seen as a title, details unverified). Why here: the project should compare tools (SAE variants, red-team methods) at equal coverage, not equal number of runs.
- **Chao et al. (2014), "Rarefaction and extrapolation with Hill numbers", Ecol Monogr 84:45-67** (seen); **Hsieh, Ma, Chao (2016), iNEXT**. Estimators accurate for rarefaction and short-range extrapolation; long-range accuracy depends on the order q and the extrapolation range. Bootstrap CIs. A later paper revised the q=1 extrapolation formula (seen; details not verified). `iNEXT` (v3.0.2), `iNEXT.3D`, `iNEXT.4steps` (sample completeness profiles) are R packages. Chao, Wang, Jost (2013, MEE 4:1091-1100) and Chao and Jost (2015, MEE 6:873-882): Shannon and q-order entropy/diversity via discovery rates of new species (seen). Why here: Hill numbers q=1,2 are estimable even when q=0 is not; mass-weighted measures match SAE reality because rare features matter little for reconstruction.
- **Software testing/fuzzing as unseen-species estimation (Böhme and coauthors).** STADS (Böhme, TOSEM 2018, arXiv 1803.02130, seen): software testing as species discovery, residual risk via the discovery probability U(n), sample coverage C(n) = 1 - U(n), and extrapolation; bootstrap CIs. "Estimating residual risk in greybox fuzzing" (Böhme, Liyanage, Wüstholz, FSE 2021, seen): residual risk cannot be directly estimated in greybox campaigns because of adaptive bias, so the discovery probability is used as an upper bound; blackbox estimators systematically and substantially under-estimate the true risk. "Reachable coverage: estimating saturation in fuzzing" (Liyanage et al., ICSE 2023, seen): jackknife estimators showed the smallest bias and variance but all estimators predict false peaks (more coverage reachable after the predicted maximum). Registered report (Liyanage, Attanayake, Luo, Gopinath, ICSME 2025, arXiv 2507.17093, seen) plans synthetic programs with ground-truth reachability, with a companion NDSS Fuzzing Workshop 2026 study comparing coverage-guided and random fuzzers (no adaptive bias). Why here: closest existing methodology; the adaptive-bias warning applies to any automated red team that learns from its predecessors' attacks. Leaves open: no work I found uses *multiple independent* campaigns as capture occasions with a recapture design.
- **Technology-assisted review (Bron, van der Heijden, Feelders, Siebes), "Using Chao's estimator as a stopping criterion for technology-assisted review", arXiv 2404.01176; ACM TOIS 43(3), 2025** (seen). Conservative stopping (upper bound of the CI) with a 95% recall target reached mean recall of 95.92% (WSS 56.21%); with a 100% target the Chao (1987) conservative method averaged 98.02%, the Chao (Rivest) optimistic 98.29%. Why here: a deployed use of Chao as a stopping rule, with an empirical recall result; also shows that Chao's bound is "good enough" for recall estimation when f1/f2 are well-behaved. (Numbers from the arXiv version; journal version may differ.)
- **Moss and Hajič (2025), "The rest is silence: leveraging unseen species models for computational musicology", arXiv 2507.14638** (seen). Uses Chao1 for conservative lower bound in musicology. Weak evidence; included as an example of cross-domain use.

### 2.7 AI-adjacent prior art (direct competitors or testbeds)

- **`github.com/ParitKansal/undiscovered-failures`** (seen on GitHub, no associated paper found). Plants 30 failure subgroups (common to very rare) by flipping most training labels in tabular ML, 5 datasets and 3 model families (450 jobs), and tests Chao1/ACE for how many failure types remain undiscovered. Reported: the share of errors from unseen failure types is estimated without bias (95.6% of planted, 100% of natural cells); the number of failure types is reliable only as a lower bound. README status conflicts with the release tag (exploratory pilot versus pre-registered), so verify before citing. Why here: independent confirmation of our pilot's conclusion #2, on a different domain, and a design to mirror (planted truth, pre-registration).
- **`github.com/ParitKansal/label-error-capture-recapture`** (seen). Capture-recapture on MMLU-Redux label errors with LLM judges and confident learning; reported as a negative result: intervals were well calibrated but too wide to beat simply counting detected errors. Why here: a warning that the statistical power of capture-recapture can be too low to matter at small N; plan sample sizes.
- **Constitutional Classifiers (arXiv 2501.18837, seen)**: about 3,000 hours of human red teaming found no universal jailbreak in the target CBRN domain. Why here: a "zero discoveries" result is exactly where a Good-Turing / rule-of-three style bound on the discovery rate is the right tool, not N.
- No paper using capture-recapture or unseen-species estimators for LLM red teaming was found by the search I ran (one query). This is not a proof of absence.

### 2.8 Misidentification (matching errors)

- **Link, Yoshizaki, Bailey, Pollock (2010), "Uncovering a latent multinomial: analysis of mark-recapture data with misidentification", Biometrics 66(1):178-185** (seen). General latent-multinomial framework; basis for later work (Schofield and Bonner "Connecting the latent multinomial", Biometrics 2015, seen). Lukacs and Burnham (2005), J Wildl Manage 69(1):396-403 (seen): genotyping error correction; known to inflate N when unique genotypes are overcounted; it works poorly if detection probabilities are too low (<0.1) or too high (>0.3) (seen in a secondary source; unverified) and was criticised as not properly accounting for misidentification (Yoshizaki 2007; Link et al. 2010; Yoshizaki et al. 2011, seen as citations). A Sonoran pronghorn comparison reports low bias (<=4%) at high capture probabilities (>=0.58) but poor CI coverage (seen).
- **Population size estimation with capture-recapture in the presence of individual misidentification and low recapture, arXiv 2304.00885** and **A Bayesian parametric approach to capture-recapture with misidentification** (J Agric Biol Environ Stat, 2025, doi 10.1007/s13253-025-00717-2), both seen as links; not read.
- Why this section matters: the project's misidentification is of a different kind (features are matched by continuous similarity, so error is threshold-dependent and asymmetric between false merges and false splits), but the latent-multinomial formalism and the spurious-singleton literature in 2.2 are the nearest tools. A "ghost rate" can in principle be estimated from planted truth.

## 3. Leads, inspirations and experiment ideas

1. **Add the population-level bound to every simulation.** For each scenario compute and plot N_LB(K) = E[S] + (K-1)/K * E[f1]^2 / (2 E[f2]) (noise-free, as in my check). Report estimator error against both N and N_LB. This converts "Chao2 is biased" into a proper decomposition: gap(N, N_LB) is the model-dependent part nobody can recover; gap(N_LB, N-hat) is estimator error.
2. **Make coverage the primary endpoint in the simulations.** Estimate mass-weighted incidence coverage (Chao-Jost), report calibration plots of estimated versus true coverage, and a "K more runs" prediction versus truth (Chao 2014 / Eriksson 2026 / smoothed Good-Toulmin). Check up to t=1, 2, 4 as in Orlitsky's log n limit. This is where the pilot's heavy-tail conclusion becomes a result.
3. **Define "coverage" for SAE features in a way that matches what SAEs do.** Incidence coverage weights each feature by the probability that a random feature *activation event* belongs to a discovered feature. That is a close cousin of "fraction of activation mass explained by found features". Compare it with the unweighted fraction of features found (S/N). They diverge sharply under power-law frequency (my check: Zipf alpha=1 has coverage 0.42 at S/N = 0.03).
4. **Use cross-validation on the runs, Orlitsky style.** Estimate from K/2 runs, predict the discoveries of the held-out K/2, and compute calibration. This works on real data where N is unknown and is the best bridge between Phase 1 and Phases 2-3.
5. **Treat width as a covariate in a Huggins/Böhning model, and seeds as the capture occasions.** Run J seeds at each of several widths; the capture history of a feature is its detection across seeds within a width. Let width be a stratum, or let the logit depend on log width. Test on the synthetic generator where the true frequency of each planted feature is known: first with the covariate being the true frequency (should give about +/-1%, as in my tau=0 case), then with a noisy covariate (tau>0), and measure the bias. This delivers an informative, falsifiable experiment on the most promising advanced estimator.
6. **Nested-run diagnostic.** For SAEs of widths w1<...<wJ, compute the "width-accumulation curve" of features found by the smallest j widths; it is a species-accumulation curve in effort. Use a Good-Turing coverage estimate on that curve (effort = width) and compare with the equal-width seed curve. If the two curves disagree, nestedness is violating exchangeability. Use Chapman on same-width pairs only.
7. **Matching-threshold sensitivity as a first-class experiment.** Sweep the cosine threshold and report f1, f2, Chao2-LB, coverage, and the "ghost rate" and "merge rate" against the planted truth. Add the `breakaway_nof1` and ratio-regression estimators, which do not depend on f1, and the Chiu and Chao (2016) singleton correction. Predict from my check D that coverage will degrade gracefully (conservatively) while Chao2 will not.
8. **Ghost-rate calibration by planted twins.** Insert duplicated features with known identity and estimate the false non-match rate at the chosen threshold; then deconvolve f1 (a latent-multinomial or simple linear correction f1_true = f1_obs - ghost_rate * captures). This is the "right answer for the wrong reason" test the pilot's README calls for.
9. **Dependence by design.** Create the log-linear dependence cases deliberately: (a) two runs with the same seed and different widths; (b) runs sharing a data order; (c) negatively dependent runs (second run penalised for the first run's features, the red-team "avoid prior attacks" case); (d) learning red teams (adaptive bias à la Böhme). Verify Brenner's sign rules and the Chao et al. 2001 confound with K=2, and show how K>=3 with `dga`/`SparseMSE` partially resolves it. Include the LCMCR identifiability bound 2J* <= K in the design.
10. **Mirror the Kansal design for red teams.** Planted attack strategies with known frequency spectrum, pre-registered analysis plan, independent attacker models as occasions. Run the label-error negative result as a warning: estimate the power (CI width) before building the attack harness.
11. **Hill-number profile of an SAE.** Following Haegeman et al. and Chao-Jost, report estimated q=1 and q=2 effective feature numbers with CIs rather than q=0.
12. **Empirical calibration table ("bound ratio" curves).** From simulations across a grid of (CV, tail index, K, N), tabulate N_LB / N against observable statistics (f1/f2, coverage, S/K). At analysis time, report the range of plausible N implied by the observed (f1/f2, coverage) under a family of generating models (a Link-style sensitivity analysis rather than a point estimate).
13. **Compare to "SAE stitching" (Leask et al. 2025) as external validation on real SAEs.** Their stitching gives an independent count of features missed by small SAEs. Compare your coverage estimate for the smaller SAE with the share of features found in the larger SAEs absent from the smaller one. (This is an idea, not something I verified in the papers.)

## 4. Pitfalls and warnings

- **Never present Chao/iChao CIs as CIs for N.** They cover the bound, not N (0-3% coverage of N in my sims at sigma>=1). Label as "lower bound with CI".
- **Total N under a heavy tail is not an estimable quantity** (Link 2003; Mao and Lindsay 2007; Haegeman et al. 2013). A tight number from a parametric model (gamma, log-normal, Pareto) comes from the family, not the data.
- **Two lists cannot separate heterogeneity from dependence** (Chao et al. 2001). If a result depends on a Lincoln-Petersen pair, show the CV^2 correction N/(1+CV^2) as a sensitivity.
- **Negative dependence is the dangerous case for N** (Brenner 1995): deliberately diversified red-team runs, or runs told to avoid known findings, can produce *over*-estimates. The pilot has no case for it.
- **Adaptive runs break the sampling model** (Böhme's adaptive bias). Any automated researcher that sees earlier findings is not an independent catch.
- **Matching is not a nuisance; it defines the data.** A threshold change moves f1 and f2 directly. Deng et al. (2024): preprocessing that removes singletons invalidates Chao1/ACE; the alternative (keeping noisy singletons) inflates the estimate. Report f1, f2 explicitly with every estimate.
- **Ghosts bias N up and coverage down; merges bias both down.** Coverage's direction under errors is the safer one for safety claims, but it is still wrong; do not describe it as unbiased under matching error.
- **K=2 is not enough, and K=3 is fragile.** iChao needs K>=4; jackknife of second order is unstable at small K; ACE's "rare" cut-off of 10 is moot for K<10. Use K>=6 for anything intended as a headline.
- **Jackknife overestimates under homogeneity** (+13% to +16% in the pilot at K=10, sigma=0) and under dense data; do not call it a default "upper bound".
- **Huggins/Alho with covariates inherits all misspecification risk and treats the unseen region by extrapolating the fitted covariate relation.** My +42% overshoot with a mis-specified link is the generic failure.
- **LCMCR default truncation is non-identified for K<10** (Aleshin-Guendel 2020); `SparseMSE`/log-linear MLEs may not exist for sparse tables (Chan et al. 2021).
- **Bayesian data augmentation priors drive N** when information is weak (Link 2013).
- **Extrapolation beyond t~log n has no guarantees** (Orlitsky et al. 2016), and the Chao 2014 extrapolation for q=1 was revised later (seen; details unverified).
- **Evaluation sample sizes:** the MMLU-Redux negative result (calibrated but too-wide intervals) shows capture-recapture can be correct and useless. Check that expected CI width at the intended K and N is smaller than the decision threshold.
- **Don't trust my simulations as a replacement for the project's own.** They are quick, single-seed-family, and the CI variance formula is the classical Chao1 one, not a bootstrap or profile likelihood. The coverage failure conclusion (for N) is structural; the exact coverage values for the bound are not tight.

## 6. Estimator decision guide for this project

Always report with every estimate: K, S_obs, f1, f2 (and f3, f4), coverage, and the matching threshold.

| Violation expected | What it does | Use | Do not use | Expected bias direction | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **No violation / calibration case** (homogeneous, independent, clean identity) | Baseline | Chapman for K=2; MLE under M0/Mt for K>=3 (Rcapture) | Jackknife (overshoots, +13% to +16% at K=10 in the pilot) | None; Chapman about -1% | Use as a positive control every time |
| **Heterogeneous catchability** (some features/attacks are easy) | Items with low p never appear; f1/f2 can only reveal part of them | Chao2 / iChao2 (K>=4) as labelled lower bound; JK2 for K>=5; ACE with cut-off = K; sample-coverage estimators; Böhning-type ratio plot to check family | Chapman / Mt (biased by 1/(1+CV^2), -33% to -48% at sigma=1 to 2); Pledger/Dorazio-Royle mixtures as primary (identified only by assumption; poor in Grimm et al. 2014) | Downward, always (Chao: by construction; JK2 less so, may overshoot) | CI covers the bound only. Headline should be coverage. |
| **Heavy tail / open population** (Zipf-like frequency; unbounded set of possible findings) | N undefined or inestimable | Mass-weighted incidence coverage (Chao-Jost) with bootstrap CI; extrapolation to 2x or 3x K (Chao 2014, smoothed Good-Toulmin, Eriksson 2026); Hill q=1,2 (Chao and Jost 2015); breakaway/ratio regression as a sensitivity | Any total-N estimate, Valiant-style support-size estimate as a headline | N-based: -80% to -99% (pilot); coverage about unbiased (my check: errors of 0.01 or less); 2x extrapolation within 2-10% (Zipf) or about 15-20% low (low-p mass) | Orlitsky limit: extrapolate no further than about log n times the sample; compare tools at equal coverage (Chao-Jost 2012) |
| **Positive dependence between runs** (same family, same seed, shared training data) | Same sign as heterogeneity; runs overlap more than independent ones | Treat family as a stratum; Chao2 on one representative run per family; cross-family Chapman; log-linear/graphical models with K>=3 (`Rcapture`, `dga`); sample-coverage with CCV (Chao et al. 2001) | Pooled Chao2 across all runs as if independent (pilot: -22% for 1 family vs -2% for 6) | Downward | The pilot's scenario 3 partly mixes dependence with heterogeneity; test a genuine two-factor interaction |
| **Negative dependence** (diversity-promoting runs; later runs avoid earlier finds) | Runs overlap less than independent ones | Avoid two-list estimators; report with and without the diversification mechanism; log-linear with a negative interaction term when K>=3 | Chapman, Chao2 | Upward, possibly extreme (Brenner 1995) | Not in the pilot |
| **Adaptive / learning runs** (red teams or automated researchers that see earlier findings) | Detection probability rises with prior discoveries; Mb-type behaviour | Treat as discovery curve; coverage by blackbox estimators is biased; use bound-style outputs (upper bound on discovery probability à la Böhme); Mb or Mtb models if capture histories are individual-level | Chao on pooled runs | Coverage overestimated (blackbox risk underestimated, Böhme et al. 2021); N-type estimates ambiguous | Need independent, non-communicating runs to treat as capture occasions |
| **Nested runs** (SAE widths; one run's detections are a near-subset of the next) | Extreme positive dependence in the direction of effort; unseen mass below the smallest threshold is invisible | Treat width as effort (accumulation curve) or as a covariate; Mth/sample coverage (Lee and Chao 1994); Huggins or Böhning with log width; use same-width seeds for K | Chapman across widths (returns about the larger set); Mt (diverges at sharp thresholds) | Downward; my threshold check: Chao2 about -10%, JK2 about 0% to +7%, Mt -7% to -15% or divergent | Report the share of features that only the largest width found (that is f1 in effort space) |
| **Misidentification: false non-matches** (ghosts) | Spurious singletons | f1-free estimators (`breakaway_nof1`, ratio regression on f>=2); Chiu and Chao (2016) singleton correction; calibrate the ghost rate from planted twins; latent-multinomial models (Link et al. 2010) | Chao2, iChao2, JK1/2, Zelterman unchanged | N-based: upward (pilot: -14% to +43% at 15% ghosts); coverage: downward (my check: 0.82 to 0.665) | Coverage is the conservative headline; N-type estimates become uninterpretable |
| **Misidentification: false merges** | Distinct items counted as one | Tighten threshold, report sweep; no standard correction | Everything unchanged | Downward for N-types (pilot -4% to -13% at 5-15% merges) | Merges lower the captured count and the singletons |
| **Rich covariates exist and are predictive** (activation frequency, norm, decoder cosine neighbours) | Heterogeneity partly explained | Huggins/Alho HT (VGAM), Böhning generalised Chao/Zelterman, drpop (two lists, flexible); check residual diagnostics | Huggins as an extrapolation to a different range of covariates; Horvitz-Thompson with extreme weights | Unbiased only if covariates explain it (my check: +1%/-1% at tau=0; -32% to -45% at tau>=1; +42% under link misspecification) | Calibrate on the planted-truth generator before trusting it on real SAEs |
| **Small K (2 to 4)** | Little information | K=2: Chapman only with a CV^2 sensitivity band; K=3: Chao2 (bias-corrected) and log-linear with one interaction; K=4+: iChao2 | JK2 (K<5), LCMCR (2J* <= K), mixture models | Downward | Prefer more runs over a cleverer estimator |
| **Few features or attacks (N small, tens)** | f2 is tiny; variance large | Chao2 with +1 correction, exact/bootstrap or profile-likelihood CI; rule-of-three style bound when no recaptures; report f1/f2 counts | Large-sample CIs | Wide intervals; label-error result shows CIs can be uninformative | Run a power check before data collection |

**Defaults I would adopt:** (1) the pilot's Chapman/Chao2/iChao2/JK2 suite stays as the baseline; (2) add mass-weighted coverage with bootstrap CI and a "2x more runs" prediction as primary endpoints; (3) add the population-level N_LB to every simulation table; (4) add one covariate estimator (Böhning generalised Chao, optionally Huggins) and one dependence-aware estimator (`dga` or `SparseMSE` on K<=5 pooled groups); (5) add an f1-free estimator and the singleton correction for the matching-error studies; (6) treat any mixture or latent-class result as a labelled sensitivity analysis, not a headline.
